How caffeine interacts with sleep (educational)

Schematic only, not personalized. These diagrams show general sleep science. Your estimated blood caffeine level is on the Caffeine curve tab.

How caffeine gets in the way of sleep (the short version)

  1. While you are awake, adenosine builds up in your brain. That buildup is a big part of why you feel sleepy later.
  2. Caffeine slips into the same spots adenosine would use on A1 and A2A receptors. It does not turn the signal on. It just blocks the slot, so the sleep-pressure message is weaker for a while.
  3. When caffeine wears off, adenosine is still there (often more than before). Receptors open up again, and sleepiness can hit harder. That is one reason people talk about a late-day crash.

This app estimates caffeine in your blood (µg/mL). Higher levels usually mean more receptors blocked, but the calculator does not measure your brain directly.

How caffeine affects sleep systems (educational)

Not personalized from your inputs. The Caffeine curve tab estimates blood caffeine from your doses; this diagram shows general sleep-related pathways.

Caffeine
🧠
Adenosine (A1/A2A)

Caffeine is a non-selective adenosine antagonist. It competitively blocks adenosine at A1 and A2A receptors, which weakens sleep-drive and arousal signals.

🌙
Circadian clock

Evening caffeine can delay melatonin onset and phase-shift your body clock. That is separate from how much adenosine is blocked at bedtime.

💤
Sleep architecture

Higher estimated plasma levels correlate in studies with longer sleep latency, less slow-wave sleep, and more fragmentation. That reflects adenosine blockade and timing together.

What is the Baur line?

In Baur et al. (2024), researchers measured plasma caffeine and EEG during controlled sleep recordings. Around ~1.4 µg/mL, they reported measurable reduction in delta power, a marker of deep slow-wave sleep intensity in that lab protocol.

The dashed line on the caffeine curve marks that study finding. Broader dose–timing effects on sleep are supported by other studies, which do not use the same plasma threshold. It is not a rule for whether you personally will sleep well tonight.

How this calculator estimates caffeine in your blood

The calculator turns your doses, weight, and clearance inputs into an estimated plasma concentration (µg/mL). That is a blood-level guess, not a brain scan. It uses a simple one-compartment model: caffeine rises toward a peak, then falls off exponentially. Multiple coffees on the same day add together.

Press Calculate to see your numbers plugged into the formulas below.

1. Peak level from dose and weight

Bigger dose or smaller body usually means a higher peak in the blood.

C_max (µg/mL) = (dose in mg × 1.65) / body weight in kg

Example: 95 mg coffee, 70 kg person → (95 × 1.65) / 70 ≈ 2.24 µg/mL peak from that drink alone.

2. Clearance speed from half-life

Your half-life comes from metabolizer type plus any factors you checked (oral contraceptives, smoking, pregnancy).

k = 0.693 / half-life in hours

Example: 5 hour half-life → k = 0.693 / 5 ≈ 0.139 per hour.

3. After the peak: exponential decay

Before peak, the model ramps up linearly. After peak, concentration falls with time.

C(t) = C_max × exp(−k × t)

t is hours since that dose peaked. Food mainly shifts when the peak happens, not the half-life.

4. Several intakes in one day

Each logged drink gets its own curve. The total at any moment is the sum of all of them (superposition).

C_total(t) = sum of each intake's contribution at time t

A morning coffee plus an afternoon espresso both count toward bedtime. See the stacking breakdown on the Caffeine curve tab.

5. Half-life modifiers multiply

Base half-life is adjusted by your selections:

t½ = base × metabolizer factor × OCP (if on) × smoking (if yes) × pregnancy (if yes)

Oral contraceptives use ×1.70 (~70% longer). Smoking uses ×0.50 (faster clearance). Pregnancy uses ×2.0 in this simplified model.

6. Colored sleep zones

Bedtime level is compared to research bands (not a personal sleep promise):

  • Low: < 0.5 µg/mL
  • Moderate: 0.5 to 1.0
  • Elevated: 1.0 to 1.4
  • High: 1.4 to 2.5 (includes Baur delta-power reference ~1.4)
  • Very high: > 2.5

See how zones tie to adenosine blockade on the Sleep tab →

What changes half-life in this model:
  • CYP1A2 metabolizer type: fast (~12.5% shorter), average, or slow (~37.5% longer)
  • Oral contraceptives: ~70% longer half-life (×1.70)
  • Smoking: ~50% faster clearance (×0.5 on half-life)
  • Pregnancy: ~2× longer half-life (simplified; real values vary by trimester)
  • Body weight and food: affect peak timing and height, not half-life

Uncertainty: Two or more hormonal/lifestyle factors together (especially smoking + pregnancy, or all three with oral contraceptives) produce less predictable clearance. Discuss caffeine use with a healthcare provider if any apply.

Important health considerations

You selected smoking, pregnancy, and oral contraceptives. This tool provides approximate caffeine clearance estimates only. It is not medical advice.

  • Smoking while using oral contraceptives is associated with increased cardiovascular risk.
  • Smoking during pregnancy is harmful to the baby.
  • Combined effects on caffeine metabolism are complex and less studied; half-life estimates here are less precise.

Please discuss caffeine intake, smoking, and contraception with a qualified healthcare provider.
